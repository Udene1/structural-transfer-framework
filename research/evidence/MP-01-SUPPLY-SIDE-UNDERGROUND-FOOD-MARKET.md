# MP-01 — Supply-Side / "Underground" Inflation Investigation

## Why this branch exists

The monetary-policy reconstruction cannot treat the CPI path as if the MPR were the only economically relevant intervention.

A competing explanation is that food-price disinflation in 2025 was materially driven by increased agricultural supply, harvest timing, expanded cultivated area, imports, improved weather/production conditions, or a combination of these. The user's proposed mechanism is more specific: high 2024 food prices may have increased the incentive to plant, attracting more farmers/acreage into production; when those crops reached market in 2025, supply could have exceeded effective demand and produced local price collapses.

This is a hypothesis to test, not an established explanation.

## Initial external evidence

### Agricultural output increased

The 2025 Agricultural Performance Survey, conducted by NAERLS with the Federal Ministry of Agriculture and partner agencies, reported higher output for rice, maize, sorghum, millet, cowpea, yam and cassava compared with 2024. The reported drivers included expanded cultivated areas, improved practices and farmer resilience. citeturn1search1turn1search3

### Market-level glut is observable

Contemporaneous market reporting gives concrete examples:

- Maize prices reportedly fell 33% between February and June 2025; farmers attributed the decline partly to glut from bumper harvests in major producing states. citeturn1search4
- Jos tomato prices reportedly fell from about N31,000 per basket in December 2024 to as low as N15,000 in March 2025, with the fall attributed to increased production and market surplus. citeturn1search5
- Garri prices reportedly fell roughly 51% year-on-year in November 2025 amid cassava oversupply. citeturn1search6
- Market observations in Imo and Abia in November 2025 linked declines in rice, beans, garri and some other staples to favourable harvests, seasonal availability and transportation conditions, while some products still rose because of seasonal shortages. citeturn1search2turn1search12

These observations support investigating a supply mechanism. They do not establish that the supply response caused the national inflation decline.

## Important competing supply channels

The evidence points to several distinct channels that must not be collapsed into "farmers produced more":

1. **Expanded domestic production**
   - cultivated area;
   - yields;
   - number of active farmers;
   - crop switching;
   - irrigation/dry-season production.

2. **Harvest timing**
   - seasonal arrival of crops can reduce prices without a permanent increase in productive capacity.

3. **Imports**
   - FAO reported that increased food imports and marketing of the 2025 cereal harvest contributed to lower food inflation in September 2025. citeturn0search1
   - Nigeria's food-import policy therefore has to be separated from domestic production.

4. **Exchange-rate / logistics effects**
   - a more stable naira and lower transport/input costs can lower market prices even without large increases in physical output.

5. **Post-harvest losses / market congestion**
   - a glut can produce low farm-gate prices even while consumers experience imperfect transmission through wholesalers, transport and storage.

6. **Demand / purchasing power**
   - lower effective demand can reduce market-clearing prices. This needs to be separated from supply expansion.

## A crucial identification problem

The hypothesis "more farmers planted because prices were high in 2024" makes a temporal prediction:

> high prices / expected returns in period t → increased planting decisions → increased harvested quantity in t+1 → increased market availability → lower farm-gate/wholesale prices.

We need evidence for each link.

It is not enough to observe:

> high prices in 2024 + more output in 2025 + lower prices in 2025.

All three can occur because of weather, policy, imports, security, credit, input availability, exchange-rate changes, or normal agricultural cycles.

## What "underground" evidence means for this research

The research should move below the national CPI/MPR level and follow physical-market signals:

### Farm level

Seek:
- area planted by crop and state;
- number of farmers / farming households;
- crop choice and crop switching;
- fertilizer/seed/pesticide purchases;
- input prices;
- farm-gate prices;
- expected versus realized yields;
- planting dates;
- harvested quantities;
- farmer reports of losses/profits.

### Wholesale / market level

Seek:
- arrivals into major markets;
- market inventories where available;
- wholesale prices;
- farm-gate-to-wholesale spreads;
- transport costs;
- storage costs/capacity;
- spoilage/post-harvest losses;
- regional price dispersion;
- trader reports of glut or shortage.

### Consumer level

Seek:
- retail prices by food item and market;
- quantities purchased;
- substitution between staples;
- household purchasing power;
- food consumption changes.

### Policy / trade level

Seek:
- food import volumes and timing;
- import waivers / tariff changes;
- government grain releases;
- fertilizer/input interventions;
- agricultural credit;
- security improvements around farmland;
- road/logistics changes.

## Testable decomposition

For food inflation, build:

**Price movement**
= domestic supply effect
+ import-supply effect
+ exchange-rate/input-cost effect
+ transport/logistics effect
+ demand effect
+ seasonal effect
+ policy effect
+ weather/shock effect
+ residual.

The objective is not to force the decomposition to sum perfectly from the beginning. It is to identify measurable components and their timing.

## What would support the user's proposed farmer-incentive mechanism?

Evidence would become stronger if we find:

1. unusually high crop-specific prices in 2024;
2. contemporaneous farmer surveys showing increased expected returns;
3. increased planted area or farmer participation for those crops in 2025;
4. crop-specific production increases following the planting cycle;
5. increased physical market arrivals;
6. falling farm-gate/wholesale prices when the new harvest arrived;
7. the strongest price reversal in crops/regions with the largest supply response;
8. evidence that the effect cannot be explained mainly by imports, weather, exchange rate, or normal seasonality.

## What would weaken it?

- planted area did not increase;
- farmer participation did not increase;
- production growth was mostly yield/weather driven;
- imports account for much of the additional supply;
- price declines began before the relevant harvest;
- crops with no production increase experienced similar disinflation;
- exchange-rate/transport changes explain most of the price movement;
- household demand contraction explains the decline better.

## A major measurement warning

Nigeria rebased its CPI in 2025. The IMF notes that the food weight fell from above 50% under the old basket to about 28% under the new basket, mechanically changing measured headline inflation and complicating comparisons around the rebasing period. citeturn0search0turn0search5

Therefore:

**2024 CPI → 2025 CPI is not a clean outcome series.**

We need item-level prices and physical-market evidence alongside the CPI.

## Current finding

The "underground" branch is not speculative in the sense of lacking observable signals. There is already external evidence of:

- higher 2025 agricultural output;
- crop-specific market gluts;
- sharp falls in some farm/market prices;
- increased food imports;
- seasonal harvest effects;
- exchange-rate and transport effects.

But the proposed **high-price incentive → more farmers/acreage → 2025 oversupply → lower food prices** mechanism has not yet been demonstrated.

## Next empirical task

Build a crop-level supply-response panel for a small set of major staples:

- maize
- rice
- cassava/garri
- tomatoes
- beans

For each, reconstruct 2024 price/incentive conditions, 2025 planted area/output, harvest timing, market prices, imports, and regional evidence.

Then compare those supply movements against the timing of aggregate food disinflation.

The central question becomes:

> **How much of the 2025 food-price decline can be explained by physical supply entering Nigerian markets, independently of the monetary-policy channel?**

Only after that should we attribute any portion of the overall inflation decline to monetary tightening.
