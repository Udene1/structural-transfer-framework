# MP-01 — Monetary Tightening, Agricultural Productivity, and the Food-Supply Countermechanism

## Status

**Hypothesis branch — under investigation.**

This branch does not assume that high interest rates mechanically reduce agricultural output, nor that the 2025 food-price decline was caused by monetary policy. It tests whether the agricultural supply response is consistent with, or difficult to reconcile with, a strong monetary-transmission explanation.

## Research question

If monetary tightening was a major mechanism behind the 2025 food-price reversal, what should we observe in agriculture?

Conversely, if agricultural supply materially contributed to the reversal, did the supply expansion occur despite tighter financial conditions, and through which channel?

## User-proposed mechanism, formalized

A proposed mechanism is:

**higher interest rates / tighter credit conditions → higher cost or reduced availability of working capital and investment finance → lower agricultural investment/productivity and/or reduced labour demand → weaker, not stronger, domestic food supply.**

This creates an empirical tension worth testing if substantial agricultural output increased in 2025.

But the mechanism has important qualifications:

- many Nigerian farmers are smallholders and may not borrow at the MPR;
- informal finance, trader finance, retained earnings, household labour and government-supported credit can weaken the direct MPR → farm-production link;
- production can increase through rainfall, cultivated area, improved inputs, extension, mechanisation, security, technology or government programmes even while market interest rates are high;
- higher food prices can increase expected returns and attract labour/capital independently of formal credit;
- aggregate output can rise through yield or weather improvements without increased farmer entry.

Therefore, **2025 higher production is not by itself evidence against monetary transmission**.

## Evidence currently available

### 1. Agricultural production costs rose sharply in 2024

The 2024 NAERLS Agricultural Performance Survey reports higher production costs for all reported crops across states relative to 2023. It specifically identifies higher fertilizer and agrochemical prices and differences in farm labour and operation costs as relevant contributors.

The national examples are substantial: rice production cost rose 37.8% (₦423,400/ha to ₦583,505/ha), maize 69.7% (₦330,621/ha to ₦561,237/ha), cowpea 40.0%, sorghum 38.8%, and yam 35.6%. citeturn1search17

This establishes a difficult production-cost environment, but does not isolate interest rates as the cause.

### 2. 2025 production increased for major crops

The 2025 NAERLS APS reports increases in production and cultivated area for rice, maize, sorghum, millet, cowpea, yam and cassava relative to 2024. The existing reconstruction records, for example:

- rice area +1.4%, production +2.7%;
- maize area +0.21%, production +2.0%;
- cowpea area +0.45%, production +4.6%;
- yam area +1.78%, production +2.21%;
- cassava area +1.44%, production +1.59%.

These are observations, not causal attribution. NAERLS itself describes the 2025 outcome as reflecting improved supply conditions and interventions, but that institutional interpretation is not adopted here as the causal conclusion. citeturn0search4

### 3. Formal agricultural credit can be insulated from market rates

CBN's Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund includes an Interest Drawback Programme that reimburses 40% of the interest element for eligible loans. CBN also documents agricultural credit schemes designed to provide below-market/single-digit financing. citeturn0search0turn0search2

This means the observed policy rate cannot be translated directly into the financing cost faced by every farmer.

### 4. New evidence: formal intervention credit was not simply expanding with the tightening episode

CBN's August 2024 Monetary Policy Review provides an important exposure clue. For July–December 2023, CBN reported:

- ACGSF: ₦5.39bn, 24,667 loans guaranteed;
- ABP: ₦8.26bn, 111,111 farmers;
- CACS: ₦11.01bn, one project.

For January–June 2024, the same table reported:

- ACGSF: ₦3.77bn, 11,082 loans guaranteed;
- ABP: **nil disbursement** during the period, with ₦101.71bn repayments;
- CACS: **nil disbursement**, with ₦20.81bn repayments.

The figures are intervention-scheme flows, not total agricultural credit. They therefore cannot establish that agricultural finance as a whole contracted. But they do establish that major CBN agricultural-intervention disbursement channels were not continuously expanding into H1 2024. citeturn1search18turn0search6

This materially strengthens the financing branch: the next question is not merely whether rates were high, but whether private/commercial agricultural lending compensated for the contraction or absence of these intervention flows.

### 5. Formal credit coverage is structurally limited

CBN states that lack of access to traditional bank finance remains a major agricultural-development constraint and that less than 5% of banks' credit is granted to agriculture. It also lists multiple targeted agricultural-credit mechanisms, including ACGSF, CACS and ABP. citeturn1search1

This is important for the transfer question: a monetary-policy transmission mechanism operating through bank lending may have limited direct reach over production if a large share of farm activity is financed outside ordinary bank credit.

This is a structural observation, not evidence that monetary policy had no agricultural effect.

### 6. Credit support itself is heterogeneous

ABP is explicitly designed to link smallholder farmers to participating financial institutions and anchors, while CACS targets commercial agricultural enterprises. ACGSF covers smallholders and medium-scale farmers/agro-processors and provides guarantees to participating lenders. citeturn1search0turn0search0

Therefore “the agricultural sector” should not be treated as one financing market. The relevant empirical unit may need to be crop × farm scale × financing source × region.

## Evidence exists that higher interest rates can constrain agricultural production

A 2025 Nigerian study on agricultural finance reports a statistically significant negative relationship between higher interest rates and agricultural production. This is useful as prior evidence for the mechanism, but it is not sufficient to identify the 2024–2025 national episode.

## Stronger identification strategy

Do not ask only:

> Did food output increase while rates were high?

Ask:

### A. Financing channel
- Did farmers actually borrow?
- At what effective rates?
- Did agricultural lending volumes change?
- Did loan maturities match planting cycles?
- Did smallholders have access to formal credit?
- Did subsidized/guaranteed credit expand or contract?
- Did private-bank lending replace the decline in CBN intervention disbursement?

### B. Productivity channel
Decompose output change:

**production = cultivated area × yield**

Then determine whether 2025 growth came mainly from:
- area expansion;
- yield improvement;
- crop switching;
- multiple planting cycles;
- reduced losses;
- improved inputs;
- weather.

### C. Labour channel
Test the claim that tighter financial conditions reduce agricultural labour demand rather than assuming it.

Look for:
- farm labour costs;
- hired-worker availability;
- rural wage movements;
- mechanisation;
- household labour substitution;
- migration;
- cultivated area per worker.

### D. Incentive channel
Test:

**high 2024 food prices → expected 2025 returns → more planting / crop switching / input use**

using direct farmer evidence where possible.

### E. Market-supply timing
The decisive physical-market sequence remains:

**planting decision → production/harvest → farmgate price → wholesale price → retail price**

If prices weaken before the relevant domestic harvest arrives, the proposed domestic-supply mechanism becomes less plausible.

## Competing explanations to keep alive

1. Domestic production expansion.
2. Weather/rainfall improvement.
3. Yield improvement.
4. Seasonal harvest cycle.
5. Imports and informal cross-border trade.
6. FX stabilization and cheaper imported inputs/food.
7. Transport/fuel-cost changes.
8. Government agricultural interventions.
9. Demand compression / lower household purchasing power.
10. Monetary tightening and financial conditions.
11. Post-harvest/storage and market-clearing effects.

## Important distinction

The question is **not**:

> "Did high interest rates cause low productivity?"

The researchable question is:

> **Given the financial conditions prevailing during the 2024–2025 tightening episode, what mechanisms generated the observed change in agricultural supply, and how much of the food-price reversal can each mechanism plausibly explain?**

That formulation allows the evidence to show whether monetary tightening, agricultural supply, FX, imports, weather, demand, or combinations of these mattered.

## Current assessment

**The credit branch is not defeated, but it has changed shape.**

The new evidence gives us two simultaneous observations:

1. agricultural production increased in 2025; and
2. major CBN agricultural-intervention disbursement channels were not continuously expanding during H1 2024, while ordinary bank-credit access to agriculture is structurally limited.

That combination makes it inappropriate to infer either “tight money reduced agricultural productivity” or “agriculture was unaffected by tight money.”

The more discriminating question is now:

> **What financing actually funded the 2024 planting decisions and 2025 output expansion?**

If private credit, intervention credit, informal/trader finance, household labour, retained earnings, or other mechanisms substituted for conventional bank finance, that substitution is itself evidence about the structure through which monetary policy reaches Nigerian agriculture.

### Next empirical test

Build a financing-exposure map for 2023–2025:

**CBN intervention flows → commercial-bank agricultural lending → effective lending rates → farmer financing source → crop/area/yield response → harvest timing → farmgate/wholesale/retail prices.**

The critical missing observation is the **private/commercial agricultural-credit series**. Until that is established, the financing branch remains unresolved.

## New evidence: commercial credit is expensive under the tightening regime

The objection that private/commercial credit should not be treated as a cheap substitute for intervention credit is supported by the observed interest-rate environment, although the exact pass-through to agricultural borrowers still needs direct measurement.

CBN's Q2 2025 Economic Report reports an MPR of 27.50%, an average prime lending rate of 18.18%, and an average maximum lending rate of 29.82%. Thus commercial borrowing remained expensive even though the average prime rate was below the MPR; the maximum lending rate was above the MPR. The report also shows money-market rates around the policy corridor. citeturn0search6

CBN's April 2025 report similarly records an average prime lending rate of 18.32% and maximum lending rate of 30.11%. citeturn0search7

This matters because “private credit replaced intervention credit” cannot be treated as a frictionless explanation. If private agricultural borrowers faced rates near the prevailing commercial lending range, borrowing could remain costly even when credit was available.

There is also prior CBN evidence that policy-rate changes do pass through to commercial-bank retail rates, although with relatively slow adjustment. A CBN study using 2007–2019 monthly data found significant adjustment of retail rates to standing-facility rates, but low speed of adjustment. citeturn0search2

However, the pass-through evidence is not enough to establish the 2024–2025 agricultural effect. We still need borrower-level or sector-level evidence on actual agricultural loan rates, volumes, maturities and eligibility.

### Revised financing hypothesis

The financing branch should therefore test two possibilities simultaneously:

1. **Transmission:** high policy and market rates raised agricultural financing costs and constrained some investment/working-capital decisions.
2. **Insulation/substitution:** parts of agriculture continued operating through informal finance, retained earnings, household labour, trader/processor finance, subsidized/guaranteed schemes, or previously contracted loans whose rates were fixed or otherwise insulated.

The key empirical question is no longer whether commercial credit was “cheap.” It was not generally cheap in the observed interest-rate environment. The question is **how much agricultural production depended on expensive market credit, and what financing sources substituted for it when intervention flows weakened.**

This distinction matters for the broader monetary-transmission case: if agriculture expanded despite expensive credit because much of the relevant production was financed outside the conventional interest-rate channel, then the aggregate inflation response cannot be inferred from the policy rate alone.

## Next evidence target

Find, for 2023–2025:
- average agricultural loan rates by bank/PFI where available;
- agricultural loan volumes and outstanding balances;
- loan tenor and whether rates were fixed/floating;
- proportion of agricultural borrowers using formal credit;
- trader/input-supplier finance and other informal financing;
- crop-specific financing exposure;
- timing of loan disbursement relative to planting;
- whether farmers reduced, maintained or increased input purchases despite higher financing costs.

Only after this can we estimate whether the agricultural supply increase occurred **through** or **despite** the monetary financing channel.


## New farmer-level financing evidence

A February 2025 Food Price Baseline Survey covering 543 smallholder farmers in Ondo, Kano, Niger, Kaduna and Benue reported that 84% operated on 1–4 acre holdings, 60% financed farming through personal savings, and only 12% accessed formal credit. Lack of finance was the most commonly reported challenge, at 54%. citeturn5search0turn5search1

This is important for the monetary-transmission branch because it provides direct evidence that, in this surveyed group, farming finance was not synonymous with commercial bank borrowing. Personal savings and family labour were prominent. That creates a plausible insulation/substitution channel through which a high policy rate may have weaker direct effects on some smallholder production decisions.

However, the survey is a February 2025 cross-section of 543 farmers in five states. It does not establish the financing source used for the 2024 planting season, the interest rate actually faced by each farmer, whether savings were accumulated from the 2024 high-price environment, or whether farmers changed input use because of interest rates.

### New incentive evidence

The same survey reported that 64% of surveyed farmers felt economically better off because of recent high crop prices, while 82.5% intended to continue cultivating their primary crops. Farmers considering switching cited high input costs, pests, diseases and low yields among their reasons. citeturn5search1

This supports the existence of a price/incentive channel but does not show that high 2024 prices caused the 2025 national production increase. It suggests that **continuation and crop choice may be more important margins than farmer entry** for this episode.

The next farmer-level test should seek evidence on 2024 versus 2025 cultivated area by crop, crop switching, input quantities/expenditure, expected versus realized crop prices, financing source/effective cost, planting dates, harvest dates and yield changes.
