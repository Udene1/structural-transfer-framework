# Targeted Review: Evidence as the Bridge Between Transfer and Policy Learning

**Date:** 2026-09-22  
**Status:** First-pass targeted review; research hypothesis remains open

## Research posture

This project does **not** begin from the conclusion that policymakers repeat policies blindly, ignore evidence, or fail to adapt.

The proposition under investigation is narrower:

> A policy mechanism that appeared to work in one economic environment or earlier period may be reused in a later or different environment. The research question is whether the evidence used to justify that reuse establishes that the mechanism's relevant conditions still hold, and whether subsequent evidence changes the policy diagnosis or choice.

We therefore need to **find out what actually happened**, case by case.

## Research question

Does existing policy-transfer and economic-policy literature already provide an explicit, operational process that connects:

**observed conditions → diagnosis → candidate mechanism → policy intervention → observed response → updating**

and, when policy knowledge comes from elsewhere or another period:

**source evidence → mechanism identification → required structural conditions → target evidence → transfer decision → feedback**?

The purpose is not to assume that policymakers ignore evidence. The question is whether the literature provides a sufficiently explicit empirical audit of this chain.

## Findings

### 1. Evidence-informed policymaking exists as a framework and institutional objective

Evidence-informed policymaking is established. OECD work describes evidence use across policy design, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation, and emphasizes that evidence informs rather than mechanically determines policy.

This establishes that governments can have evidence-informed processes. It does **not**, by itself, establish that a particular policy decision was preceded by adequate evidence that a previously observed mechanism remained valid in the current environment.

That distinction is central to this project.

### 2. Policy-transfer literature already contains an evidence/learning route

A systematic review of 180 empirical studies by van de Wetering et al. identifies transfer routes including imitation, adaptation, inspiration, and a learning route. The learning route can involve extensive policy evaluation or piloting and is described as less vulnerable to incomplete or uninformed transfer. The authors nevertheless note that factors shaping transfer are context-dependent and that further operationalization is needed. [1]

Therefore **evidence-informed transfer is not a new idea**.

### 3. The empirical operation of learning remains testable

A later review of 65 transport-policy papers found that policy learning was frequently invoked, but relatively few studies clearly defined or empirically measured learning. It reports that the literature still knows relatively little about how learning triggered by other contexts is transformed into action. [2]

This does not prove that policymakers fail to learn. It tells us that **learning needs to be operationalized and observed if we want to test it**.

### 4. Adaptive policymaking already formalizes monitoring and adjustment

Adaptive-policy literature explicitly designs monitoring systems and mid-course corrections. Dynamic adaptive policy pathways use monitored signals and trigger values to determine when actions should be altered, stopped, expanded, or activated. [3][4]

Again, this establishes an available methodology. It does not establish that every real-world policy process follows it.

### 5. Economic diagnostics already formalizes observation → diagnosis → intervention

An IMF guidance note on growth diagnostics describes a sequence in which observed facts are used to identify likely constraints; a causal story is formulated and subjected to testable implications that can be confronted with data; only then does analysis move toward policy interventions. The note also recognizes political-economy constraints affecting the policy response. [5]

Therefore we cannot claim that the sequence itself is novel.

## The question we now need to test

Consider a simple repeated-policy case:

**Period 1**

A policy is implemented.

Observed response appears consistent with a mechanism.

**Period 2**

The same or similar policy is implemented again.

But between the two periods, potentially important conditions have changed:

- demographics;
- income distribution;
- financial depth;
- banking structure;
- exchange-rate regime;
- fiscal conditions;
- expectations;
- market structure;
- international integration;
- technology;
- institutions;
- political constraints;
- external shocks;
- measurement systems.

The research question is **not**:

> Why did policymakers repeat the policy?

Nor:

> Did policymakers blindly repeat the policy?

It is:

> **What evidence was used to establish that the mechanism remained applicable when the policy was reused, and what happened to that assessment when new evidence arrived?**

That is observable in principle.

## Proposed empirical audit

For each transferred or temporally reused policy mechanism, record:

1. **Source observation:** What outcome/problem was observed?
2. **Source diagnosis:** What causal explanation was proposed?
3. **Source evidence:** What evidence supported that explanation?
4. **Mechanism:** Through what channel was the policy expected to operate?
5. **Required structures:** What institutional, market, behavioural, informational, financial, or other conditions were necessary?
6. **Target observation:** What problem/outcomes were observed in the target environment?
7. **Target diagnosis:** What evidence supported the same or a different causal explanation?
8. **Structural correspondence:** Which required conditions were present, absent, changed, or uncertain?
9. **Decision evidence:** What evidence was actually available to policymakers when the intervention was selected?
10. **Intervention:** What policy was selected and why?
11. **Implementation:** Was the intended intervention actually delivered?
12. **Response:** What happened after implementation?
13. **Alternative explanations:** What shocks, behavioural responses, measurement problems, or competing mechanisms could explain the response?
14. **Updating:** Did new evidence change the diagnosis, confidence in the mechanism, or policy choice?
15. **Transfer assessment:** supported / conditional / contradicted / indeterminate.

The final assessment must be evidence-based, not assumed in advance.

## What would count as evidence against our hypothesis?

We should actively look for this.

Examples:

- policymakers explicitly diagnosed the changed environment before reusing the policy;
- they estimated or tested the relevant transmission mechanism using current data;
- they identified structural changes and incorporated them into the decision;
- they used experiments, forecasts, counterfactual analysis, or other evidence to determine whether the mechanism remained applicable;
- subsequent evidence led them to revise the diagnosis or intervention;
- apparent repetition turns out to be a different mechanism under the same policy instrument.

If the evidence shows this, our proposed concern becomes weaker.

## What would count as evidence supporting the concern?

Also examples, not assumptions:

- policy rationale relies primarily on the historical success of the same intervention;
- relevant structural changes are observable but not considered in the decision process;
- the assumed mechanism's empirical conditions have materially changed;
- new evidence contradicts the mechanism but policy persists without an identifiable evidence-based explanation;
- the apparent repeated policy is actually associated with a different mechanism, but the distinction is not recognized;
- policy adjustment occurs only after large failures rather than through predefined evidence-based updating.

Even then, we would need to distinguish this from political constraints, implementation problems, data limitations, uncertainty, or rational persistence.

## Current novelty assessment

**Not established as novel.**

The literature contains strong prior art for:

- policy transfer and learning;
- context-sensitive adaptation;
- evaluation-driven learning;
- adaptive monitoring;
- economic diagnostics;
- causal transportability.

The remaining candidate is an **empirical integration question**, not a claim that we discovered evidence-informed policymaking.

The sharper possible contribution is a case-level audit that follows:

**evidence supporting a source mechanism → structural conditions → target evidence → policy decision → implementation → observed response → updating.**

Whether that integration is genuinely absent, already supplied by existing methods, or simply a useful application protocol remains to be determined.

## Implication for the Nigeria case

The Nigeria monetary-policy case should therefore begin without assuming either success or failure of transfer.

For a historical/current monetary-policy episode, we should reconstruct:

> What mechanism was being relied upon?  
> What evidence supported it?  
> What conditions made the mechanism operate previously?  
> Which of those conditions still existed?  
> Which had changed?  
> What evidence was available when the decision was made?  
> What happened afterward?  
> Did the new evidence change the diagnosis or policy?

The case could produce:

- evidence of appropriate reuse;
- conditional transfer;
- structural mismatch;
- implementation failure;
- competing-mechanism explanation;
- evidence of adaptive updating;
- evidence of persistence despite changed conditions;
- or simply insufficient evidence.

We do not choose the result beforehand.

## Decision

Do not claim that policymakers are evidence-blind.

Do not claim that evidence-informed policymaking guarantees evidence-validated mechanism reuse.

**Find out what happened.**

The next research task is to test actual historical policy episodes against this audit and, in parallel, determine whether existing procedural frameworks already perform the same operation.
