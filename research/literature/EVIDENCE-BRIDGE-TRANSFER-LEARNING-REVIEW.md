# Targeted Review: Evidence as the Bridge Between Transfer and Policy Learning

**Date:** 2026-09-22  
**Status:** First-pass targeted review

## Research question

Does existing policy-transfer and economic-policy literature already provide an explicit, operational process that connects:

**observed conditions → diagnosis → candidate mechanism → policy intervention → observed response → updating**

and, when policy knowledge comes from elsewhere:

**source evidence → mechanism identification → required structural conditions → target evidence → transfer decision → feedback**?

The purpose is not to assume that policymakers ignore evidence. The question is whether the literature provides a sufficiently explicit empirical audit of this chain.

## Findings

### 1. Policy-transfer literature already contains an evidence/learning route

A systematic review of 180 empirical studies by van de Wetering et al. identifies transfer routes including imitation, adaptation, inspiration, and a learning route. The learning route can involve extensive policy evaluation or piloting and is described as less vulnerable to incomplete or uninformed transfer. The authors nevertheless note that the factors shaping transfer are context-dependent and that further operationalization is needed. [1]

This is important: **evidence-informed transfer is not a new idea**.

### 2. The unresolved issue is the empirical operation of learning

A later review of 65 transport-policy papers found that policy learning was frequently invoked, but few studies clearly defined or empirically measured learning. It reports that the literature still knows relatively little about how learning triggered by other contexts is transformed into action. [2]

This supports a narrower research question: not whether learning exists, but whether we can trace and test the evidence-to-action-to-updating process.

### 3. Evidence-informed policymaking explicitly includes feedback

Recent OECD work treats evaluation results and feedback mechanisms as components of an evidence-informed policymaking ecosystem. UNESCO similarly describes evidence use across policy-cycle stages and identifies broken feedback loops as a barrier to effective evidence use. [3][4]

Therefore, **evidence + feedback + policy learning is established literature**, not a novel concept.

### 4. Adaptive policymaking already formalizes monitoring and adjustment

Adaptive policy literature explicitly designs monitoring systems and mid-course corrections. Dynamic adaptive policy pathways use monitored signals/trigger values to determine when actions should be altered, stopped, expanded, or activated. [5][6]

Therefore, **monitoring → feedback → adjustment is also established**.

### 5. Economic policy already has a diagnostic-to-policy formulation

This is especially important for the economic research programme. An IMF guidance note on growth diagnostics describes a sequence in which observed facts are used to identify likely constraints; a causal story is then formulated and subjected to testable implications that can be confronted with data; only then does the analysis move from diagnosis to policy interventions. The note also stresses that the appropriate policy mix depends on political-economy constraints beyond the diagnosis itself. [7]

This is very close to the proposed:

**observation → diagnosis → causal mechanism → intervention**.

Consequently, we cannot claim that this sequence itself is a new contribution.

### 6. The transfer-specific bridge remains the sharper question

The literature reviewed so far contains the components separately:

- policy transfer / adaptation / learning;
- evidence-informed policymaking;
- evaluation and policy learning;
- adaptive monitoring and feedback;
- economic diagnostics;
- causal transportability.

The research gap, if one exists, must therefore be narrower than any one of these.

The candidate question is:

> **When a policy mechanism is learned from another environment or historical period, what evidence is required to establish that the mechanism's operative structural conditions are present in the target environment, and how should subsequent observations update the transfer assessment?**

This formulation joins two already-established processes rather than claiming either process is new.

## Proposed empirical audit to test against prior art

For each transferred or temporally reused policy mechanism, record:

1. **Source observation:** What outcome/problem was observed?
2. **Source diagnosis:** What causal explanation was proposed?
3. **Source evidence:** What evidence supported that explanation?
4. **Mechanism:** Through what channel was the policy expected to operate?
5. **Required structures:** What institutional, market, behavioural, informational, financial, or other conditions were necessary?
6. **Target observation:** What problem/outcomes are observed in the target environment?
7. **Target diagnosis:** What evidence supports the same or a different causal explanation?
8. **Structural correspondence:** Which required conditions are present, absent, changed, or uncertain?
9. **Intervention:** What policy was selected and why?
10. **Implementation:** Was the intended intervention actually delivered?
11. **Response:** What happened after implementation?
12. **Alternative explanations:** What shocks, behavioural responses, measurement problems, or competing mechanisms could explain the response?
13. **Updating:** Did new evidence change the diagnosis, confidence in the mechanism, or policy choice?
14. **Transfer status:** supported / conditional / contradicted / indeterminate.

The final category must be evidence-based, not a score or assumed verdict.

## Current novelty assessment

**Not established as novel.**

The review finds strong prior art for every major component:

- policy transfer and learning;
- context-sensitive adaptation;
- evaluation-driven learning;
- adaptive monitoring;
- economic diagnostics;
- causal transportability.

What remains potentially distinctive is the **integration as a case-level empirical audit**, particularly one that:

1. begins with the evidence supporting a mechanism in the source environment;
2. explicitly identifies the structures required for that mechanism;
3. tests those structures against target evidence;
4. separates structural-transfer failure from implementation failure;
5. follows the mechanism after intervention;
6. records how subsequent evidence changes the transfer assessment.

This is a candidate research operation, not yet a novelty claim. It must next be compared directly with realist CMO analysis, mechanism-based policy-transfer frameworks, causal transportability/selection diagrams, and institutional-transplantation methods.

## Implication for the Nigeria case

The Nigeria monetary-policy case should not begin with the proposition that a mechanism was blindly transferred.

Instead, it should ask:

> What policy mechanism was being relied upon, what evidence supported that mechanism in the relevant source or historical environment, what structural conditions were required for transmission, what evidence shows whether those conditions existed in Nigeria during the relevant period, and what happened after implementation?

This allows the case to produce any of several results: successful transfer, conditional transfer, structural mismatch, implementation failure, competing-mechanism explanation, or insufficient evidence.

## Decision

The project should **not** claim to have discovered evidence-informed transfer, adaptive policymaking, economic diagnostics, or policy learning.

The next research task is to test whether the proposed audit is already fully supplied by existing procedural frameworks, or whether the literature leaves an operational gap at the exact junction:

**source mechanism evidence ↔ structural requirements ↔ target evidence ↔ post-intervention updating.**
