# Evidence, Diagnosis, Learning, and Adaptive Policy — Literature Review

**Status:** First-pass literature review, 2026-09-22  
**Role in programme:** Investigate a user-generated research question without treating the observation as an empirical conclusion.

## Research question

A new candidate question has emerged:

> When policymakers select an economic policy mechanism, how much of the decision process is driven by current evidence and diagnosis of the observed environment, versus inherited theory, established policy routines, historical evidence, or conventional solutions?

This is a **research question, not a claim** that policymakers routinely ignore data or apply textbook economics verbatim.

A stronger operational formulation is:

> What evidence can establish whether policy selection and adjustment respond to the current observed economic environment, and under what conditions does policy practice incorporate learning from new data?

## Why this matters to the structural-transfer programme

The question adds a second possible failure point to the existing transfer framework.

The existing transfer question is:

**Source environment → policy mechanism → target environment**

The new question asks what happens before and after the mechanism is selected:

**Observed environment → diagnosis → candidate mechanism → intervention → observed response → updating**

A policy outcome can therefore differ from expectations for multiple reasons:

- incorrect diagnosis;
- incorrect causal mechanism;
- structural mismatch between mechanism and environment;
- implementation failure;
- measurement problems;
- unexpected behavioural adaptation;
- regime change;
- external shocks;
- source-model error;
- insufficient learning or delayed updating.

The research must distinguish these rather than attributing an outcome to any one explanation.

## Literature found

### 1. Evidence-informed policymaking is already an established field

GAO's 2023 evidence-building guidance explicitly treats data, evaluations, performance information, and research as inputs into policy management and emphasizes assessment, evidence use, and continuous improvement. It also reports mixed progress in collecting, analyzing, and using evidence effectively. This establishes that the broad question "does government use evidence?" is not new.

Source: U.S. GAO, *Evidence-Based Policymaking: Practices to Help Manage and Assess the Results of Federal Efforts* (2023).

### 2. Economic theory already contains adaptive-learning formulations

Svensson and Williams (2007) model policymaking when the true economic structure is unobserved and policymakers learn from observations. Their adaptive-policy formulation explicitly permits policymakers to update beliefs.

Source: NBER Working Paper 13414, *Bayesian and Adaptive Optimal Policy under Model Uncertainty*.

### 3. Monetary economics has explicitly studied learning and experimentation

A 1993 *Journal of Monetary Economics* paper, *Learning, experimentation, and monetary policy*, models policymakers learning under uncertainty and argues that experimentation can generate information useful for future policy decisions.

Therefore we cannot claim that adaptive experimentation is absent from monetary economics.

### 4. Development economics has directly studied context-sensitive evidence

Pritchett and Sandefur (2015), *Learning from Experiments When Context Matters*, examine the trade-off between evidence from experiments conducted elsewhere and observational evidence from the target context. Their result is directly relevant to the present research direction: contextual evidence can sometimes be more informative for a target policy question than experimental evidence transported from another setting.

### 5. Policy experimentation is an established empirical subject

Wang and Yang (2021) study policy experimentation and learning in China and examine how experimentation affects policy learning and outcomes. The literature therefore already provides examples of governments using experimentation as a learning mechanism.

### 6. Evidence-to-policy translation is itself studied

A 2020 *World Development* article, *From experimental findings to evidence-based policy*, examines how experimental findings move through the policy cycle and how institutional mechanisms and government structures affect whether evidence changes policy.

An Annual Review of Political Science review (2019) likewise argues for combining theory with contextual knowledge and evaluating explanations, not merely whether policies work.

## What this rules out

We should **not** claim:

- policymakers do not study data;
- economists merely implement textbook rules;
- conventional policy is necessarily inferior to experimentation;
- adaptive policy is necessarily superior;
- Nigeria is an example of any of these failures before empirical testing.

Those are hypotheses or possible explanations to test.

## More precise candidate research boundary

The interesting unresolved question is narrower:

> **Can we empirically audit the link between observed economic conditions, the diagnosis of the operative mechanism, the selection of a policy intervention, and subsequent updating when new evidence arrives?**

This could connect naturally to the existing transfer audit:

1. What was observed?
2. What diagnosis was made?
3. What causal mechanism was assumed?
4. What evidence supported that mechanism?
5. What structural conditions were assumed?
6. What intervention followed?
7. What happened after implementation?
8. What alternative explanations exist?
9. What evidence became available afterward?
10. Did the diagnosis, mechanism belief, or intervention change in response?

The final question is especially important. **Policy adjustment itself can become observable evidence about the learning process.**

## Important methodological constraint

The framework must allow the data to show that conventional policy selection was justified.

Possible empirical outcomes include:

- evidence-responsive policy selection;
- evidence-responsive but constrained policy selection;
- delayed updating;
- conflicting evidence;
- justified reliance on established theory;
- inappropriate persistence of a mechanism after its conditions changed;
- successful adaptive experimentation;
- failed experimentation;
- insufficient evidence to distinguish among these.

No outcome is assumed in advance.

## Connection to temporal transfer

This makes the temporal-transfer problem more precise.

Instead of asking:

> Why are old monetary-policy ideas still being used?

we ask:

> **What evidence justifies treating a historically identified policy mechanism as transportable from an earlier economic environment into the current one?**

That can be tested by reconstructing historical episodes, identifying the conditions under which the mechanism operated, measuring changes in those conditions, and examining whether the transmission relationship persists.

## Current assessment

The broad concept of evidence-based, adaptive, experimental, and learning-oriented policymaking is clearly established.

The possible research contribution therefore cannot be "policymakers should use data" or "policymakers should experiment."

The remaining candidate contribution is a **diagnostic/empirical audit of policy selection and updating**, potentially integrated with the structural-transfer audit. Whether that integration is genuinely distinct from existing frameworks remains unresolved.
