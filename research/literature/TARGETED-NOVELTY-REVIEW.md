# Targeted Novelty and Boundary Review — Paper 1

Status: Active review; novelty **not established**.

## Purpose

Test the candidate gap against the closest literature before defining the contribution of Paper 1.

The candidate question under review is:

> Can preservation or alteration of the underlying causal/evidence-generating structure of a policy mechanism be made an explicit object of policy-transfer analysis?

This review is deliberately adversarial. We should prefer an existing framework over claiming to have invented one.

## Finding 1 — Policy transfer already contains causal-mechanism analysis

Policy-transfer scholarship does not treat transfer as a purely descriptive copying process. Existing work identifies mechanisms such as learning, competition, emulation and coercion, and later work uses actor-centred and mechanism-based approaches to explain how transfer occurs.

A 2025 review by Samuel Henderson is especially important. It argues that negative transfer cases remain under-studied and explicitly calls for greater attention to causal mechanisms and the pathways through which negative cases arise.

Implication:

We cannot claim to have invented "causal mechanisms in policy transfer" or "mechanism-based analysis of failed transfer."

Source:
Henderson, S. (2025 online). "Defining negative cases of policy transfer." Policy Studies. DOI: 10.1080/01442872.2025.2478884.

## Finding 2 — Causal transportability already formalizes source-to-target mechanism differences

Transportability research is an even closer methodological predecessor.

Mehrotra, Petersen & Geng (2019) explain causal transportability as a framework for applying causal findings from one setting to another while explicitly representing contextual differences and the causal mechanisms through which an intervention affects an outcome. Selection diagrams represent where data-generating processes may differ between source and target settings.

This directly overlaps with several elements of the proposed framework:

- source/target distinction;
- causal mechanism representation;
- contextual differences;
- identification of differences that matter for the causal path;
- explicit assumptions;
- testable implications.

Implication:

We cannot claim to have invented the idea that causal structure matters for whether evidence transfers across settings.

Source:
Mehrotra, M. L., Petersen, M. L., & Geng, E. H. (2019). "Understanding HIV Program Effects: A Structural Approach to Context using the Transportability Framework." Journal of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndromes. DOI: 10.1097/QAI.0000000000002202.

Degtiar & Rose (2023) provide a broader review of generalizability and transportability.

Source:
https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-statistics-042522-103837

## Finding 3 — Policy transfer literature and transportability appear to solve different parts of the problem

The literature suggests a potentially useful boundary rather than a proven gap.

Policy-transfer research commonly studies:

- why policies travel;
- who transfers or receives them;
- what is transferred;
- how transfer is adapted;
- political and institutional constraints;
- adoption, implementation and negative cases.

Causal transportability commonly studies:

- whether a causal effect identified in a source population can be validly applied to a target population;
- which differences between source and target affect the causal estimate;
- what assumptions and measurements are required for valid transport.

The two literatures therefore have substantial conceptual overlap but different primary objects of analysis.

A possible Paper 1 contribution could be a disciplined bridge between them for economic-policy analysis: not a new claim that mechanisms or structure matter, but a framework for tracing a specific economic policy mechanism from source evidence to target structure and distinguishing several reasons why transfer evidence may or may not hold.

This remains a **candidate contribution**, not an established novelty claim.

## Finding 4 — "Context matters" is far too broad

Existing work already provides formal ways to represent contextual differences. The transportability literature specifically warns against treating context as one undifferentiated variable and instead identifies contextual characteristics that modify parts of the causal path.

Therefore, Paper 1 should not present "context matters" as its contribution.

The stronger question is operational:

> Which structural conditions are required for a specified policy mechanism, and which of those conditions are actually present in the target environment?

That formulation is still subject to further literature testing.

## Finding 5 — Negative transfer is a promising empirical boundary, but not an invented concept

Henderson's 2025 review is directly relevant because it identifies negative transfer as insufficiently studied and calls for stronger causal-mechanism analysis.

This gives the programme a useful research direction:

- do not begin with successful policy transfer;
- do not begin with failure either;
- construct cases where transfer can be evaluated and classify the evidence after mechanism and structure have been examined.

Potential outcome categories remain:

1. Supported transfer.
2. Conditional/partial transfer.
3. Structural mismatch.
4. Transfer failure.
5. Insufficient evidence.

These categories are working research categories, not claims about the literature.

## Finding 6 — The current "structural transfer" language needs a stricter definition

The term should not merely mean "a policy works differently in another country."

A provisional definition worth testing is:

> Structural transfer analysis examines whether the structures and causal relationships that are necessary for a specified policy mechanism to generate its claimed effect in a source environment are sufficiently preserved, transformed, or replaced in a target environment.

This definition contains several testable components:

- policy mechanism;
- claimed outcome;
- source evidence;
- mechanism assumptions;
- necessary structures;
- enabling structures;
- target correspondence;
- observed target operation;
- competing explanations.

It is provisional and should be revised if existing literature provides a better formulation.

## Current novelty status

**Not established.**

The review has ruled out several overly broad novelty claims:

- not "policy transfer is understudied";
- not "causal mechanisms in policy transfer are new";
- not "context affects transfer";
- not "formal institutions can differ from functional outcomes";
- not "causal effects may fail to transport";
- not "negative transfer deserves study."

The most defensible candidate contribution currently appears to be a possible **integration and operationalization problem**:

> whether the policy-transfer and causal-transportability traditions can be combined into an empirically usable framework specifically for evaluating the preservation, transformation, or breakdown of economic-policy mechanisms across structurally different environments.

Even this statement requires further checking against integrated frameworks before being treated as a contribution.

## Decision rule

If an existing framework already provides the bridge, adopt it and move on to the empirical research question.

If no framework provides the bridge, do **not** immediately call it novel. First specify exactly what is missing, why that missing component matters, and what new empirical test the proposed framework makes possible.
