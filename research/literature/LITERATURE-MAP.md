# Literature Map — Paper 1

Status: Initial map; not exhaustive.

## Policy transfer
Dolowitz & Marsh (2000), "Learning from Abroad: The Role of Policy Transfer in Contemporary Policy-Making." This is a direct predecessor. It defines policy transfer around using knowledge about policies, administrative arrangements, institutions and ideas from one setting in another, and asks what is transferred, by whom, why, to what degree, what constrains transfer, and how successful it is.

Relevance: We cannot claim novelty for "policy transfer." Our possible distinction is whether the underlying causal/evidence-generating structure required by a policy mechanism survives transfer.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1111/0952-1895.00121

## Policy diffusion
Newmark (2002), "An Integrated Approach to Policy Transfer and Diffusion." Reviews the overlap between transfer and diffusion.

Relevance: We need to distinguish how a policy travels from whether the causal structure supporting it travels.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1541-1338.2002.tb00269.x

## Institutional transplantation
Berkowitz, Pistor & Richard (2003), "Economic Development, Legality, and the Transplant Effect." Cross-country evidence suggests that how law is transplanted and received matters for effective legality; adaptation and familiarity matter.

Relevance: Formal institutional similarity does not guarantee functional equivalence.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0014-2921(01)00196-9

Seidler (2014), "When do institutional transfers work? ... the case of Borno in north-eastern Nigeria." Develops a transplant-effect framework and applies it to a Nigerian case.

Relevance: Important prior work. Paper 1 must clearly distinguish structural transfer from transplant-effect concepts.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1017/S1744137414000046

Seidler (2017), "Copying informal institutions: the role of British colonial officers during the decolonization of British Africa."

Relevance: Formal institutional copying can produce different practical outcomes when informal institutions and implementation conditions differ.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0010417516000316

## Context-sensitive development policy
Rodrik (2008), "Second-Best Institutions." Argues that appropriate institutions may need to account for context-specific market and government failures rather than simply adopting best-practice institutions.

Relevance: Close to our policy-fit intuition, but not identical to a mechanism-level structural-transfer test.

Source: https://doi.org/10.3386/w14050

A 2020 Policy and Society special issue on transnational policy transfer emphasizes adaptation, translation, power relations, political will and institutional differences in development cooperation.

Relevance: Confirms that transfer in development settings is already understood as context-sensitive.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1080/14494035.2019.1626078

## External validity and transportability
Degtiar & Rose (2023), "A Review of Generalizability and Transportability." Reviews methods for extending causal inferences from source populations to target populations and emphasizes target-population differences and treatment-effect heterogeneity.

Relevance: Major methodological analogue. We should learn from its explicit source/target assumptions.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-statistics-042522-103837

"External Validity" in Annual Review of Political Science systematizes external-validity questions and emphasizes credible inference beyond the original setting.

Relevance: Supports making source-to-target inference an explicit research object.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-041719-102556

## Initial synthesis
The literature search changes our starting point. We are not asking an untouched question called "does policy transfer work?"

Existing research already covers policy transfer, diffusion, institutional transplantation, transplant effects, adaptation/localization, context-dependent institutions, external validity and causal transportability.

The candidate research gap is narrower:

Can these literatures be connected through a framework that treats preservation or alteration of the underlying causal/evidence-generating structure of a policy mechanism as the object of transfer?

This is a candidate gap, not an established novelty claim.

## Immediate questions
1. Does policy-transfer scholarship explicitly model the causal mechanism that makes a transferred policy work?
2. Does it distinguish formal policy representation from the underlying mechanism?
3. Can transportability concepts handle institutional and causal-system changes rather than mainly population/effect-modifier differences?
4. Is there already a framework combining policy transfer, institutional transplantation and causal transportability?
5. How is "context" operationalized in comparative political economy?
6. Can structural correspondence be made empirically testable?
7. How can implementation failure be distinguished from mechanism-transfer failure?


## Targeted novelty and boundary review

### Negative policy transfer and causal mechanisms
Henderson (2025 online; Policy Studies 2026 issue) systematically reviews negative cases of policy transfer and reports that negative cases remain under-studied. Importantly, the article identifies greater attention to causal mechanisms as a direction for improving understanding of the pathways producing negative transfer.

Relevance: this rules out claiming that mechanism-focused analysis of negative transfer is itself new. It also makes negative-transfer cases a direct adjacent literature that Paper 1 must engage.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1080/01442872.2025.2478884

### Causal transportability as a structural source-target framework
Mehrotra, Petersen & Geng (2019) apply causal transportability to context-dependent intervention effectiveness. They explicitly represent causal mechanisms, source/target differences, and data-generating-process differences using selection diagrams, with testable implications for what must be measured or assumed to transport an effect.

Relevance: this is a close methodological predecessor. It rules out claiming that source/target causal structure, contextual differences, or mechanism preservation are new ideas by themselves. The remaining question is whether this machinery has been adequately connected to policy-transfer analysis and economic-policy institutions.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1097/QAI.0000000000002202

### Updated synthesis
The candidate gap has narrowed substantially. The research programme should no longer ask whether policy transfer has been studied causally or whether context affects transfer. Both are established.

The live research question is now whether an empirically usable framework can integrate:

- policy-transfer analysis;
- mechanism-level causal analysis;
- source-to-target transportability;
- institutional/economic structural conditions;
- and explicit differentiation between implementation failure, mechanism failure, source-model error, and insufficient evidence.

This remains a candidate integration problem, not a novelty claim.
