# Third Integration Review — Structural Transfer Boundary

**Status:** Phase 0 active; integration review completed at first-pass level. No novelty claim established.

## Purpose

Test the remaining candidate contribution against the closest combination of:

- policy transfer and policy diffusion;
- causal transportability;
- mechanism-based policy analysis;
- contextual validity;
- monetary-policy transmission research;
- fiscal-policy transmission and fiscal federalism research.

The question is no longer whether context, mechanisms, or structural differences matter. Existing literature clearly establishes all three.

The narrower question is:

> Does existing scholarship already provide an empirically usable procedure for tracing a specified economic-policy mechanism from source evidence, through its required structures, to target correspondence while distinguishing mechanism-transfer failure from implementation failure?

## Finding 1 — Monetary-policy transmission already studies structural heterogeneity

Cross-country monetary-policy research directly conditions transmission on structural characteristics.

Deb et al. (2023) find significant cross-country and time heterogeneity in monetary-policy transmission and relate effectiveness to characteristics including exchange-rate regime, financial-system development, policy credibility, uncertainty, financial conditions, and fiscal-policy coordination.

Tiedemann, Bizimana & Tougouma (2026) find substantial heterogeneity across Sub-Saharan African emerging and frontier economies and investigate monetary-policy transparency, financial development, fiscal dominance, the sovereign-bank nexus, and exchange-rate flexibility as structural factors.

These studies establish that economic-policy transmission is already studied as conditional on macro-financial structure.

**Implication:** Paper 1 cannot claim that economic-policy mechanisms are normally treated as context-free or that structural conditions have been ignored.

## Finding 2 — Fiscal-policy research likewise conditions transmission on structural characteristics

Fiscal-multiplier literature explicitly examines how country characteristics alter transmission.

The IMF's fiscal-multiplier synthesis identifies structural characteristics such as trade openness, debt, financial conditions and monetary accommodation as determinants of multiplier size.

Woldu (2023) studies fiscal multipliers and structural economic characteristics across Sub-Saharan African countries, including trade openness, debt, exchange-rate regime, financial development and political regime.

Gupta & Dubey (2025) find nonlinear relationships between fiscal multipliers and structural characteristics such as financial infrastructure, human development and import propensity.

**Implication:** The research programme cannot claim invention of the proposition that fiscal-policy effects depend on structural characteristics.

## Finding 3 — Existing research is strong on conditional effects, but that is not identical to a transfer-audit protocol

The economic-policy transmission literature usually asks questions such as:

- how large is the effect?
- under which structural characteristics is it larger or smaller?
- which transmission channels operate?
- how does the effect vary across countries or states?

The proposed research question is narrower and procedural:

1. identify the source policy mechanism;
2. identify the causal conditions on which the source evidence depends;
3. classify those conditions as necessary, enabling, or incidental where evidence permits;
4. determine which conditions correspond in the target;
5. test whether the target mechanism actually operates;
6. separate implementation differences from a broken or altered mechanism;
7. preserve competing explanations and uncertainty.

This difference is potentially useful, but it is **not yet a novelty claim**. A further review is required to determine whether policy-transfer, realist evaluation, transportability, or comparative institutional research already provides this exact protocol.

## Finding 4 — Nigeria is already a rich empirical setting for structural institutional analysis

Existing Nigerian fiscal-federalism research has examined the relationship between formal federal structure and actual intergovernmental fiscal relations.

For example, historical work documents revenue dependence and centralization among Nigerian states, while IMF analysis has examined risk-sharing and coordination across government tiers.

This means Paper 3 should not ask merely whether Nigeria is formally federal. It should specify the fiscal mechanism being tested and identify the structural conditions that mechanism requires.

## Current boundary

The research question has narrowed from:

> Do imported policies fit different countries?

to:

> Can we establish, with evidence, whether a specified economic-policy mechanism retains the structural conditions required for its claimed effect when applied in a different economic environment?

That formulation is still provisional.

## What has now been ruled out as a contribution

We should not claim novelty for:

- policy transfer;
- mechanism-based policy-transfer analysis;
- negative transfer;
- context dependence;
- causal transportability;
- structural heterogeneity in monetary-policy transmission;
- structural heterogeneity in fiscal multipliers;
- formal institutions differing from functional outcomes.

## Remaining unresolved problem

The potentially unresolved object is a **mechanism-level transfer audit** for economic policy:

> a case protocol that joins source-policy evidence, causal mechanism, required structures, target correspondence, observed target operation, implementation conditions, and competing explanations in one auditable chain.

This is the current research boundary, not a confirmed contribution.

## Required next step

Before formalizing Paper 1, inspect the closest frameworks that may already provide this protocol:

1. realist evaluation / CMO configuration analysis;
2. mechanism-based policy transfer;
3. causal transportability using DAGs and selection diagrams;
4. institutional transplantation and transplant-effect analysis;
5. comparative policy-effectiveness frameworks;
6. economic-policy transmission studies that explicitly map channels to structural conditions.

If an existing framework already provides the required chain, use it rather than inventing a new one.

If the chain is fragmented across frameworks, define precisely what integration is missing and what empirical test the integration enables.
